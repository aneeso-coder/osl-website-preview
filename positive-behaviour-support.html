What Positive Behaviour Support is

Positive Behaviour Support (PBS) starts from a simple idea: behaviour that other people find difficult is telling us something. It is communicating an unmet need, a mismatched environment, pain, fear, or a situation the person has no control over.

That reframing sounds soft. It is not. It is the difference between asking “how do we stop this behaviour?” and asking “what is this behaviour achieving for this person, and how else could they achieve it?” The first question leads to restriction. The second leads somewhere useful.

What PBS means here, in practice

We change the environment, not the person

We work out what the behaviour is achieving, then change the environment and the support so the person can get that outcome another way. Very often the environment is the problem: too much noise, too little warning, too many people, no way to say no, or a routine built around staff convenience rather than the person.

We teach skills rather than manage incidents

Managing incidents is what you do when you have run out of ideas. Teaching someone a reliable way to communicate what they need — and making sure the people around them respond to it consistently — is what actually reduces incidents. That is slower, and it works.

We measure whether restriction is going down

A PBS plan that does not reduce restrictive practice over time is not working, however good it sounds on paper. The measure of the plan is whether the person’s life is getting less restricted, not whether the paperwork is up to date.

Restrictive practice: our position

Physical intervention is a last resort, not a plan.

Our staff are trained in personal safety interventions by an accredited in-house trainer — our Registered Manager, Dawn Newby, who is an accredited staff trainer in personal safety measures for behaviour that may challenge.

Every restrictive practice should have four things: a start date, a reason, a review date, and a plan to end it. A restriction with no end date is not a support plan. It is a habit that nobody has questioned, and it should be treated as a problem to be solved rather than a permanent feature of someone’s life.

Training that sits inside the organisation

Our training is delivered in-house rather than bought in as an online tick-box. That matters for PBS specifically: a plan is only as good as the consistency of the people delivering it, and consistency comes from training that can be shaped around the actual person and refreshed quickly when their needs change.

Alongside personal safety interventions, our accredited trainers deliver CPR, first aid, choking response, safe defibrillator use, moving and handling, and epilepsy awareness with buccal rescue medication.

Where PBS fits

PBS is how we approach behaviour across our service — most often alongside learning disability and autism support, and sometimes alongside mental health support. Our Nominated Individual, Dr Bal Powar, is an NHS consultant psychiatrist whose background includes autism and neurodevelopmental community services, Care and Treatment Reviews, and community forensic mental health services.

The building matters too. A person placed in a home that overwhelms them will have their distress recorded as behaviour. See Property Solutions.
